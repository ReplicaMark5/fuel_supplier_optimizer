***How Volume Tiers Work***

Two main structures are real and widely used:

Step-up / incremental (marginal litres get the higher rebate).
→ This is the standard practice in global majors (Shell, BP, TotalEnergies, Chevron, etc.), not something I’ve made up.
→ You’ll find this described in downstream fuel sales contracts, wholesale petroleum supply agreements, and even in Shell’s own published pricing models in some markets (they sometimes call them “volume incentive discounts”).

Minimum commitment / all-units (must hit a threshold to qualify, or once you cross, all litres get the higher rebate).
→ Common with refiners/regionals (Astron, Engen, Sasol in South Africa, Caltex in Asia, etc.).
→ Often written into agreements as “rebate is subject to a minimum of X million litres uplifted per annum” or “if uplift exceeds Y, a rebate of Z shall apply to all litres”.

COC and payment-term rebates are a separate, standard commercial lever:

Collect vs. delivery = logistics saving.

Cash vs. 30/45/60 = financing cost recovery.

These rebates stack with (or are gated by) volume tiers in every petroleum supply agreement I’ve seen.

So the logic I gave is not invented — it’s a synthesis of how actual supply agreements are structured.

⚠️ What varies (and you must check in your contracts/data)

Retroactivity: Some “step-up” tiers are incremental only; others are “all-units” once threshold is passed. Both are used in practice.

Aggregation: Tiers may be per-product, per-depot, per-contract, or aggregated at account (all depots + all fuels).

Eligibility vs. bonus: With some suppliers, the tier just unlocks the right to get your COC rebate; with others, the tier itself adds extra cents per litre.

Clawbacks: If you don’t meet the minimum annual volume, some majors claw back the rebate retroactively at year-end.
